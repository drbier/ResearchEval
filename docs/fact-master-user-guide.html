Part 1

Using F.A.C.T.

Chapter 1

1. Quick Start

F.A.C.T. turns a medical paper into a structured critical appraisal in which every claim points back to the exact paragraph, table or figure it came from. You can be reading your first appraisal in about two minutes.

  1. Sign in. Use the Sign In button at the top right. New accounts are reviewed by an administrator before they unlock (see Chapter 8).
  2. Pick a mode. The switcher at the top has three modes: Find, Analyze and Contextualize. Talk (asking questions) lives inside Analyze once a paper is open.
  3. Give it a paper. In Analyze, either upload a PDF (📄 PDF tab) or paste a PubMed link, PMID, PMC ID or DOI (🔗 Link / PMID tab) and press Analyze or Fetch & Analyze.
  4. Read, click, ask. The analysis appears on the left. Click any citation pill, such as [Res, p6], to jump to the source paragraph on the right.
[Screenshot: the F.A.C.T. header showing the Find / Analyze / Contextualize switcher and the PDF / Link input bar]

The four verbs

🔍 Find

Describe a study in plain language, even a vague recollection, and get real, verified papers from PubMed and Europe PMC.

🔬 Analyze

Get a structured appraisal matched to the paper's study design, with every claim anchored to its source location.

📝 Contextualize

Combine 3–10 papers into one unified clinical briefing, so you see how the evidence fits together.

💬 Talk

Ask follow-up questions about an open paper (for example, “What was the blinding method?”). Answers cite their sources.

Typical workflow: Find a paper → click Analyze Full Text on its card → read the appraisal → Ask follow-ups → add several papers to Contextualize for a combined briefing.

Chapter 3

3. Full Text vs. Abstract

An appraisal of a full paper and an appraisal of an abstract are very different things: an abstract can't show the full methods, the randomization and blinding details, the complete harms tables, or the statistical analysis plan. For that reason, F.A.C.T. never analyzes an abstract unless you explicitly say so.

3.1 How F.A.C.T. looks for the full text

When you give it a link, PMID or DOI, F.A.C.T. tries these sources in order and uses the first that returns a genuine PDF or full-text article:

1. PubMed Central (structured full-text XML) ↓ not available / blocked 2. Unpaywall (every known open-access copy: repositories, preprint servers) ↓ none usable 3. OpenAlex (open-access locations across the scholarly index) ↓ none usable 4. Europe PMC (open-access full text) ↓ none usable 5. STOP and ASK YOU → upload PDF / analyze abstract only

3.2 Case A — Publisher Download Restriction

Some publishers make a paper free to read in your browser but refuse automated downloads, so it can't be fetched for you. F.A.C.T. detects this and shows a dialog with:

  • A direct link to open the article on PubMed Central.
  • A 🎓 Find on Scholar ↗ button for alternate free copies.
  • Two choices: 📤 Upload PDF Manually or Analyze Abstract Only.
  1. Click the link to open the article, then download the PDF in your browser.
  2. Click 📤 Upload PDF Manually. F.A.C.T. switches to the PDF input and opens your computer's file picker straight away.
  3. Choose the PDF you just downloaded. You get a full-text analysis.

3.3 Case B — Paywalled paper

If no free full text exists anywhere, the dialog Full Text Unavailable (Abstract Only) shows the official abstract and asks whether to analyze the abstract only. Press Cancel if you would rather obtain the paper first.

Abstract-only analyses are clearly marked. The generated source document is stamped [ABSTRACT-ONLY EVALUATION] at the top, so it can't be mistaken for a full-text appraisal later.

[Screenshot: the “Publisher Download Restriction” dialog with the PMC link, Find on Scholar, Upload PDF Manually and Analyze Abstract Only buttons]

3.4 Using Google Scholar to find another copy

Sometimes Google Scholar lists a free copy (a university repository, an author's site) that PubMed does not. F.A.C.T. gives you a one-click way to look:

  1. Click 🎓 Scholar Versions ↗ on a Find card, or Find on Scholar in the dialog. Google Scholar opens in a new tab.
  2. The paper you want is normally the top result. Click All N versions beneath it to see every copy.
  3. Download the PDF, then use 📤 Upload PDF Manually.

Google Scholar does not allow software to read its pages automatically, so F.A.C.T. can open the search for you, but the “All N versions” click has to be yours.

Chapter 4

4. Find Papers

The Verified Literature Finder searches PubMed and Europe PMC using natural language. Every result is verified against the published abstract.

4.1 Searching

  1. Switch to 🔍 Find.
  2. Type what you know, in your own words. For example: “CoQ10 reduced cardiovascular mortality in elderly Swedish cohort, 5 year follow up.” You do not need exact titles or perfect terminology. Partial recollections work.
  3. Press Find Verified Papers (or Enter). Two example chips under the box fill in sample queries.

Results are cached for 24 hours so repeat searches are instant. Tick Bypass 24h Cache (Force Refresh) to search fresh.

5.1 The study-type badge

Once the paper is read, a badge appears at the top right naming the study design F.A.C.T. identified (for example, Randomized Controlled Trial). Hover over it to see what that design means and which methodological rules are being applied to this paper.

5.2 The sections of an appraisal

The headings are matched to the study design. For a randomized trial, you will see:

5.3 Source anchors: check every claim

Each factual claim carries a small citation pill, such as [Res, p6] or [Disc, p1]. Click it and the right-hand Source Evidence Inspector scrolls to the exact passage and highlights it with a glowing box. You never have to take a claim on trust. Click and read the original.

Chapter 6

6. Talk: Ask the Paper

Switch to the 💬 Ask tab in the left panel to question the open paper. Type a question and press Enter or Send ➤.

  • What was the blinding method?
  • How many patients were excluded after randomization?
  • Did the authors pre-specify the primary endpoint?

Answers cite the same coordinate anchors as the analysis, so each one links to the passage it came from.

Ask is limited to the paper you have open. If you opened an abstract-only analysis, it can only answer from the abstract.

Chapter 7

7. Contextualize

Contextualize (the Multi-Study Evidence Synthesis panel) combines several papers into one unified clinical briefing.

  1. Switch to 📝 Contextualize.
  2. Paste 3–10 PubMed URLs, PMIDs or DOIs, one per line. Papers you added from Find are already there. A counter shows Batch size: N / 10.
  3. Press 🚀 Generate Science Briefing.
  4. Read the Synthesized Briefing Document, and use 📋 Copy to Clipboard to take it elsewhere.

Contextualize works from structured abstracts, not full text. It is excellent for seeing how a body of evidence lines up, but for the detailed critical appraisal of any one paper, run that paper through Analyze.

8. Settings & Access

8.1 Signing in and approval

F.A.C.T. is a registered-clinician tool. After you sign in, a new account shows Access Authorization Pending while an administrator reviews it. That screen unlocks automatically the moment you are approved, with no refresh needed. Until then, you can't analyze papers or run searches.

8.2 Settings & Preferences

Open the gear icon at the top right to personalize:

  • Appearance: Light Mode or Dark Mode.
  • Default startup view: which mode F.A.C.T. opens in.
  • Highlight Color: the color used for citation pills and the highlight box on the source paper (default Warm Amber). A live preview shows the effect.
  • 5. Clinician Voice Profile & Exemplars (F.A.C.T. 2.0):
    • Therapeutic Lens: Choose Integrative / Naturopathic / Functional (default), Conventional Allopathic, or Methodological / Biostatistical.
    • Voice DNA Directives: Calibrate Core Clinical Philosophy, Preferred Terms, and Prohibited Boilerplate. Use the Extract Voice Directives AI tool to auto-derive them from your writing.
    • Exemplar Writing Library: Add up to 10 past newsletters or commentaries (single or batch import separated by ---) so F.A.C.T. mirrors your sentence rhythm and tone.

9. Why PubMed & Europe PMC, Not Google Scholar

F.A.C.T.'s search is built on PubMed and Europe PMC rather than Google Scholar. Scholar is a good general discovery tool (that is why we link to it for finding copies), but for appraising clinical evidence, a curated biomedical index has real advantages.

Clinical needPubMed & Europe PMCGoogle Scholar
Source vettingBiomedical journals indexed under editorial and scientific selection (MEDLINE).Automated crawler. Indexes whatever looks like an academic document.
Subject indexingPapers tagged with controlled MeSH terms, so related terminology is found together.Keyword matching and citation counts.
ReproducibilityA given query is transparent and repeatable.Ranking is a black box and can vary between users.
Study designPublication types (RCT, meta-analysis, etc.) are searchable fields.No design filters.
CorrectionsRetractions and errata are flagged on the record.Retracted papers can remain indexed without warning.
PreprintsEurope PMC labels preprints as not peer reviewed.Preprints and journal articles appear in one blended list.
Full-text dataStructured full text (XML) for open-access papers, which F.A.C.T. can read directly.Links out to publisher pages and PDFs.
Chapter 10

10. Why Everything Is Verified & Abstract-Only Is Never Silent

10.1 “Verified” in Find

Language models can invent plausible-sounding papers. F.A.C.T. avoids this by design: candidate papers come from PubMed and Europe PMC records, and each one is checked against its published abstract before it is shown. The papers you see exist, the links go to real records, and the summaries are drawn from the paper's own abstract. If neither database has a match and a result comes from the Semantic Scholar fallback, it is labeled as such.

10.2 Why you are always asked about abstracts

The risk: an abstract-only appraisal looks authoritative but can't check the things that matter most for bias: the randomization method, allocation concealment, blinding, attrition, the full harms data, and the statistical analysis plan.

So the rule is simple: if a full-text copy exists, F.A.C.T. uses it. If it can't be fetched, F.A.C.T. tells you why and gives you the choice. You can upload the PDF yourself or accept the weaker, abstract-only analysis, and the result is stamped as abstract-only. The decision is always yours, and it is never made quietly on your behalf.

10.3 Why every claim has an anchor

An appraisal you can't check is just another opinion. Anchors make each statement auditable in seconds: click, see the passage, decide for yourself whether the paper supports the claim.

Chapter 11

11. Study Types & Appraisal Frameworks

A good appraisal of a diagnostic-accuracy study asks different questions from one of a randomized trial. F.A.C.T. first identifies the design, then applies methodological rules appropriate to that design. It does not apply one generic checklist to everything.

11.1 The ten study types

Randomized Controlled Trial

Superiority, parallel or multi-arm interventional trials.

Non-Inferiority RCT

Trials showing a treatment is not unacceptably worse than an active comparator.

Systematic Review & Meta-Analysis

Systematic searches with pooled effect estimates.

Observational / Real-World Evidence

Cohort, case-control and registry studies.

Diagnostic Accuracy Study

Performance of tests, imaging and screening tools.

Biomarker Study

Prognostic and predictive molecular or circulating markers.

Health Economic Evaluation

Cost-effectiveness, cost-utility and budget-impact analyses.

Clinical Trial Protocol / SAP

Pre-specified protocols and statistical analysis plans.

Survey Research

Clinician or patient attitude and practice surveys.

Narrative Review

Expert commentary and state-of-the-art summaries.

11.2 Where the rules come from

F.A.C.T.'s appraisal rules are drawn from recognized methodology and reporting standards, including RoB 2, CONSORT (including its harms extension), SPIRIT, PRISMA, STARD, STROBE, REMARK, CHEERS and GRADE. Each of the 45 rules is traced to a specific source document and page in the reference library the system was built on, so the standard behind each criterion is documented, not assumed.

Matching the right rules to the right design is why a trial's appraisal asks about randomization and blinding, while an observational study's appraisal asks about confounding. The study-type badge shows you which was applied.

13.2 Frequently asked questions

Does F.A.C.T. ever analyze just an abstract without telling me?

No. Whenever only an abstract is available, whether from a paywall or a publisher block, F.A.C.T. stops and asks. Analysis proceeds only after you click Analyze Abstract Only, and the source document is stamped as abstract-only.

The paper is free in my browser, so why won't F.A.C.T. fetch it?

Some publishers allow people to read a paper but block software from downloading it. F.A.C.T. detects this and tells you. Open the link it gives you, download the PDF yourself, then press 📤 Upload PDF Manually.

Can I trust the papers that Find returns?

Results come from PubMed and Europe PMC records and are checked against each paper's abstract, so they are real papers with working links. Pay extra attention to any marked Semantic Scholar Fallback. It means neither database matched.

How do I know the appraisal is accurate?

Click the citation pills. Each claim links to the exact passage in the paper. If the passage does not support the claim, you can see that immediately. Treat the appraisal as a structured aid to your own judgment.

Why did Find give me different results from Google Scholar?

They search different things. Find searches curated biomedical records and checks them. Scholar searches the open web. See Chapter 9. For free copies of a paper, use the Scholar Versions button.

Can I analyze a paper that isn't on PubMed?

Yes. Upload the PDF in the 📄 PDF tab, or paste a DOI or a direct link to the PDF.

What is the difference between Analyze and Contextualize?

Analyze is a deep, single-paper critical appraisal (full text where available). Contextualize is a broad synthesis across 3–10 papers, based on their abstracts.
