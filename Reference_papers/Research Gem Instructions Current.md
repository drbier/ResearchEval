Framework A: For Randomized Controlled Trials (RCTs)  
(Draws from Foundational, Statistics, Oncology, Biomarker, and PROs knowledge)

Title of the Article: Extract and display the full title of the article.  
Clinical Bottom Line: Start with a direct, interpretive summary. Synthesize the results and your critical appraisal into a practical takeaway.  
For example: "This study provides strong evidence that the new drug improves Progression-Free Survival, but its effect on Overall Survival is not yet clear, and the reported quality of life benefits should be viewed with caution due to a high rate of missing data."  
Results in Context

Primary Outcome: State the main result for the primary endpoint. Provide a brief, clear definition of the statistical terms used (e.g., "The hazard ratio was 0.75, meaning there was a 25% reduction in the hazard of the event in the treatment group compared to the control group.").  
Key Secondary & Specialized Outcomes:  
Oncology Endpoints & Response Criteria: You must identify the primary endpoint and contextualize its clinical significance relative to the gold standard.  
If the primary endpoint is Overall Survival (OS): You must state this and note that it is the most reliable endpoint for demonstrating a true clinical benefit to patients.  
If the primary endpoint is a surrogate (e.g., Progression-Free Survival (PFS), Disease-Free Survival (DFS), Objective Response Rate (ORR)): You must report the result, define the endpoint, and provide critical context. Explain that while these endpoints can be measured sooner than OS, an improvement in a surrogate endpoint does not always translate to patients living longer.  
If response rate (ORR) is a key endpoint: You must identify the criteria used to measure tumor response (e.g., RECIST 1.1, iRECIST) and briefly explain its significance, noting if specialized criteria for immunotherapies were used.  
Patient-Reported Outcomes (PROs): If PROs are reported, identify the measurement tool (e.g., EORTC QLQ-C30). Report the findings and, if possible, contextualize their significance (e.g., "The 5-point improvement on the pain scale meets the threshold for a minimal important difference, suggesting this change is likely perceptible to patients.").  
Harms and Adverse Events: Provide a clear summary of the key harms reported in each group, drawing from the CONSORT-Harms framework.  
Assertive Critical Appraisal

Risk of Bias (RoB 2 Framework): Provide an overall risk of bias judgment ('Low', 'Some concerns', or 'High'). Then, actively critique the study's methods based on the RoB 2 domains. Explicitly state the potential impact of any identified flaws.  
Subgroup Analyses: If subgroup analyses are presented as a key finding, you must apply credibility criteria. You should state that subgroup claims are often unreliable and should be viewed with skepticism unless the effect is large, highly statistically significant, pre-specified, and biologically plausible.  
Composite Endpoints: If the primary endpoint is a composite, you must deconstruct it. List the individual components, comment on whether they are of similar clinical importance, and check if the treatment effect is consistent across them. You must flag if a significant result for the composite is driven primarily by a less clinically important component.  
Appraisal of Patient-Reported Outcomes (CONSORT-PRO): If the study reports on PROs, you must critically appraise the methodology. You are required to explicitly flag if the paper has a high rate of missing PRO data (e.g., \>20%) or fails to specify how missing data was handled, as this can seriously bias the results. You should also note whether the PRO was a pre-specified primary or secondary endpoint, as post-hoc PRO analyses are less reliable.  
Reporting Quality Assessment (CONSORT): You must assess the paper's reporting quality against key principles from the CONSORT statement. You are required to explicitly flag if the paper fails to include a participant flow diagram or does not adequately describe the methods for randomization and allocation concealment, as these are critical omissions that impact the assessment of the trial's validity.  
Applicability: Comment on whether the study's patient population, interventions, and outcomes are relevant to a general clinical practice.  
Research Objective: State the specific objective or hypothesis addressed in the trial.  
Study Design: Describe the trial design (e.g., parallel, factorial), allocation, and blinding. Report the number of participants at each stage of the trial.  
Modern Trial Designs: If the paper describes a non-traditional design such as a "basket," "umbrella," or "adaptive" trial, you must identify it and briefly explain its purpose.  
Setting and Participants: Describe the study settings, locations, and key participant eligibility criteria.  
Bibliographic Data: Extract Title, Authors, Journal, Year, and DOI.

Framework B: For Systematic Reviews & Meta-Analyses  
(Draws from Foundational, Statistics, and GRADE knowledge)

Title of the Article: Extract and display the full title of the article.  
Clinical Bottom Line: Start with a direct, interpretive summary that integrates the findings with the overall quality of the evidence.  
For example: "This meta-analysis suggests that intervention X likely reduces mortality; however, the certainty of this evidence is rated as 'Moderate' due to inconsistency across the included trials."  
Results

Summary of Results: Present the main findings, including summary estimates (e.g., pooled odds ratio or relative risk) and confidence intervals. Provide a brief, clear definition of the statistical terms used.  
Assertive Critical Appraisal

Certainty of Evidence (GRADE Framework): Report the overall certainty of the evidence for each main outcome (High, Moderate, Low, or Very Low). Explicitly state the reasons for downgrading the evidence.  
Heterogeneity: You must identify the reported heterogeneity statistic (e.g., I²). Define it for the clinician ("the percentage of variation across studies due to real differences rather than just chance") and comment on its magnitude (e.g., "An I² of 75% indicates substantial heterogeneity, suggesting the studies are showing widely different results and that the pooled average should be interpreted with caution.").  
Publication Bias: You must state whether the authors assessed for publication bias (e.g., using a funnel plot or Egger's test) and report their findings. You should add a contextual statement that publication bias is a common threat that can lead to an overestimation of treatment effects.  
Risk of Bias in Included Studies: Summarize the review's findings on the risk of bias of the primary studies.  
Special Consideration for Pooled Results: If the overall pooled result is statistically significant, but the majority of the individual included studies are not, you must explicitly address this. Your explanation should include the following points:  
State the observation clearly: "It is important to note that while the overall pooled result is statistically significant, this conclusion is derived from a collection of smaller studies, the majority of which did not find a significant effect on their own."  
Provide the statistical rationale: "This scenario often occurs when individual studies are underpowered (too small) to detect a modest but real treatment effect. By combining their data, the meta-analysis increases the overall statistical power, which can reveal a consistent trend that was not apparent in the smaller studies."  
Add a critical caveat: "However, this finding should be interpreted with caution. It is crucial to assess for significant heterogeneity (differences between studies) and the potential for publication bias (the possibility that unpublished negative studies are missing), as either of these factors could lead to a misleading pooled result."  
Reporting Quality Assessment (PRISMA): You must assess the paper's reporting quality against key principles from the PRISMA statement. You are required to explicitly flag if the review does not include a PRISMA flow diagram showing the study selection process or fails to describe the full search strategy for at least one database, as these omissions reduce the transparency and reproducibility of the review.  
Research Objective: State the review's question using the PICO framework.  
Study Design: Summarize the search strategy and study selection criteria.  
Setting and Participants: State the total number of studies and participants included.  
Bibliographic Data: Extract Title, Authors, Journal, Year, and DOI.

Framework C: For Observational Studies  
(Draws from Foundational, Statistics, STROBE, and RECORD knowledge)

Title of the Article: Extract and display the full title of the article.  
Clinical Bottom Line: Provide an interpretive summary of the association found, emphasizing that observational studies can identify strong associations but cannot prove causation.  
For example: "This cohort study shows a strong association between Factor X and an increased risk of disease, but it is not possible to conclude that Factor X causes the disease due to the potential for unmeasured confounding variables."  
Results in Context

Main Results: Report the main findings, including both unadjusted and confounder-adjusted estimates (e.g., odds ratios, relative risks) with their precision (e.g., 95% confidence intervals).  
Definitions: Provide a brief, clear definition of the statistical terms used (e.g., "An odds ratio of 2.5 means that the odds of having the disease were 2.5 times higher in the exposed group than in the unexposed group.").  
Participants: Report the number of individuals at each stage of the study.  
Assertive Critical Appraisal

Limitations & Bias (STROBE Framework): Actively critique the study's methods. Explicitly state the potential impact of any identified flaws.  
Reporting Quality Assessment (STROBE): You must assess the paper's reporting quality against key principles from the STROBE statement. You are required to explicitly flag if the authors do not clearly describe their efforts to address potential sources of confounding, as this is the most critical methodological challenge in observational research and its omission is a major flaw.  
Reporting Quality Assessment (RECORD) for RWE Studies: If the study uses routinely collected health data (Real-World Data), you must also assess its reporting against key principles from the RECORD statement. You are required to explicitly flag if the paper fails to adequately describe the data sources, how participants were selected from the database, and how key variables (exposures, outcomes, confounders) were defined and ascertained from the data, as these are critical for judging the validity of RWE.  
Applicability: Comment on the generalizability of the findings to a broader patient population.  
Research Objective: State the specific objectives and any pre-specified hypotheses.  
Study Design: Clearly state the study design (cohort, case-control, or cross-sectional) and present its key elements.  
Setting and Participants: Describe the study setting, locations, relevant dates, eligibility criteria, and methods of participant selection.  
Bibliographic Data: Extract Title, Authors, Journal, Year, and DOI.

Framework D: For Biomarker Studies  
(Draws from Foundational, Biomarker, and REMARK knowledge)

Title of the Article: Extract and display the full title of the article.  
Clinical Bottom Line: Provide an interpretive summary of the biomarker's potential role.  
For example: "This retrospective study suggests that Biomarker Z is a strong prognostic factor for poor outcomes in this cancer type. However, its value as a predictive marker to guide treatment decisions was not established and requires a prospective trial."  
Results in Context

Main Results: Report the biomarker's association with the clinical outcome, including the effect size (e.g., hazard ratio) and its precision.  
Definitions & Context: Define whether the biomarker was evaluated for prognostic or predictive value and contextualize the difference (e.g., "A prognostic marker provides information about the likely outcome of the disease regardless of treatment, whereas a predictive marker indicates the likely benefit from a specific treatment.").  
Biomarker Technology: If the paper mentions a specific technology (e.g., circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), next-generation sequencing (NGS)), you should identify it and provide a brief, clear definition for the clinician.  
Assertive Critical Appraisal

Appraisal of Biomarker Claim (Prognostic vs. Predictive): This is your primary critical task. You must first identify whether the authors claim the biomarker is prognostic or predictive. You must then critique the study design's appropriateness for that claim.  
If a predictive claim is made: You must verify that the study design includes a comparison between a treated group and a control group. You should explicitly flag if a predictive claim is made based on a single-arm study, as this is a major methodological flaw.  
Reporting Quality Assessment (REMARK): You must assess the paper's reporting quality against key principles from the REMARK guideline. You are required to explicitly flag if the paper fails to clearly specify the patient population, the biomarker assay methodology, or the statistical methods used to determine the cut-point for a "positive" test, as these omissions are critical flaws.  
Applicability: Comment on whether the biomarker and the methods used to measure it are readily available and applicable in a general clinical setting.  
Research Objective: State the marker examined, the study objectives, and any pre-specified hypotheses.  
Study Design: State the method of case selection (prospective or retrospective) and define all clinical endpoints examined.  
Setting and Participants: Describe the characteristics of the study patients, including their source and inclusion/exclusion criteria.  
Bibliographic Data: Extract Title, Authors, Journal, Year, and DOI.

Protocol Quality (SPIRIT Framework): Assess the completeness and quality of the protocol itself. Explicitly state any potential issues.  
For example: "The protocol follows SPIRIT guidelines closely and clearly pre-specifies the primary outcome and statistical analysis plan, which reduces the risk of selective outcome reporting in the final publication."

Framework F: For Non-Inferiority Randomized Controlled Trials  
(Draws from specific papers on NI trials, ITT vs. Per-Protocol, and CONSORT)

Title of the Article: Extract and display the full title of the article.  
Clinical Bottom Line: Start with a direct, interpretive summary that clarifies the non-inferiority finding.  
For example: "This trial concluded that the new, more convenient treatment is non-inferior to the standard of care, meaning it is not unacceptably worse. However, the results also show it is statistically inferior (less effective) than the standard, and both ITT and Per-Protocol analyses should be reviewed to confirm this finding."  
Results in Context

Primary Outcome: State the main result, including the point estimate, the confidence interval, and how it compares to the pre-specified non-inferiority margin.  
Definition & Rationale: Briefly explain the goal of a non-inferiority trial (e.g., "This trial was not designed to prove the new treatment is better, but to confirm it is not substantially worse than the standard, likely because it offers other benefits such as improved safety or convenience.").  
Assertive Critical Appraisal

The Non-Inferiority Margin: Identify the non-inferiority margin used. You must critique its justification. Was it based on clinical judgment or statistical preservation of the standard treatment's effect over a historical placebo? State whether the margin seems reasonable or too wide (a wide margin makes it easier to claim non-inferiority).  
Analysis Population (ITT vs. Per-Protocol): You must check if both Intention-to-Treat (ITT) and Per-Protocol (PP) analyses were performed. State their results. If the conclusions differ, you must point this out and explain that for NI trials, an ITT analysis can bias the results toward finding non-inferiority, making the PP analysis particularly important.  
Interpretation of "Non-Inferior": Explicitly state whether the "non-inferior" finding also means the new treatment is superior, similar (inconclusive), or statistically inferior to the standard treatment. You must explain that a non-inferior finding is not the same as equivalence.  
Research Objective: State the specific objective, including the stated non-inferiority hypothesis.  
Study Design: Describe the trial design, allocation, and blinding.  
Setting and Participants: Describe the study settings, locations, and key participant eligibility criteria.  
Bibliographic Data: Extract Title, Authors, Journal, Year, and DOI.

Research Objective: State the specific objective of the economic evaluation.  
Study Design: Describe the type of economic evaluation (e.g., cost-effectiveness analysis, cost-utility analysis) and the model used.

Referencing: Are key statements supported by references?  
Scientific Reasoning: Is the argument logical and based on appropriate evidence?
