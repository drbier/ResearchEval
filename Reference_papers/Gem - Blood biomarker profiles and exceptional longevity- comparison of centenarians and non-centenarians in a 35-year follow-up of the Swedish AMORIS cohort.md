Blood biomarker profiles and exceptional longevity: comparison of centenarians and non-centenarians in a 35-year follow-up of the Swedish AMORIS cohort

### **Clinical Bottom Line**

This large prospective cohort study demonstrates an association between favorable metabolic, renal, hepatic, and inflammatory biomarker profiles measured from age 64 onward and survival to age 100\. The strongest relative differences were observed for lower serum uric acid and creatinine, alongside lower fasting glucose and modestly higher total cholesterol and iron. Because this is an observational study using administrative health register linkage, these biomarker patterns indicate strong biological associations but cannot establish causal pathways due to unmeasured residual lifestyle confounding, reverse causality in the comparator cohorts, and survival selection bias.

### **Results in Context**

* **Main Results:**  
* 

  * **Uric Acid:** Demonstrates a distinct dose-response association; individuals in the highest quintile (Q5: 389.7+ \$\\mu\$mol/L) had an adjusted odds ratio (OR) of 0.61 (95% CI, 0.47 to 0.78) for reaching age 100 relative to the middle quintile (Q3: 291.3–332.6 \$\\mu\$mol/L). Those in the lowest quintile had nearly double the chance of becoming a centenarian compared to those in the highest quintile.  
  *   
  * **Creatinine:** Higher levels were inversely associated with achieving centenarian status; participants in Q5 (101.1+ \$\\mu\$mol/L) exhibited an adjusted OR of 0.56 (95% CI, 0.44 to 0.70) relative to Q3 (81.1–89.0 \$\\mu\$mol/L).  
  *   
  * **Glucose:** Elevated fasting blood glucose was associated with lower odds of surviving to 100 years (Q5: 6.0+ mmol/L; OR 0.67; 95% CI, 0.53 to 0.85 vs Q3: 5.0–5.3 mmol/L).  
  *   
  * **Liver Function Markers:** Higher values in Q5 were inversely associated with reaching 100 for aspartate aminotransferase (ASAT; OR 0.69; 95% CI, 0.56 to 0.86), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT; OR 0.69; 95% CI, 0.54 to 0.89), alkaline phosphatase (ALP; OR 0.73; 95% CI, 0.58 to 0.91), and lactate dehydrogenase (LD; OR 0.75; 95% CI, 0.60 to 0.94).  
  *   
  * **Total Cholesterol & Iron:** Higher total cholesterol showed an inverse risk at the lowest quintile (Q1: \$\\le\$5.2 mmol/L; OR 0.80; 95% CI, 0.64 to 1.00 vs Q3: 5.9–6.4 mmol/L). Lower iron was similarly associated with reduced survival to 100 (Q1: \$\\le\$13.0 \$\\mu\$mol/L; OR 0.73; 95% CI, 0.60 to 0.88 vs Q3: 15.1–18.0 \$\\mu\$mol/L).  
  *   
  * **Alanine Aminotransferase (ALAT) & Albumin:** Did not display statistically significant associations with reaching age 100 across quintiles (e.g., Albumin Q5: OR 1.00; 95% CI, 0.80 to 1.24).  
  *   
* **Definitions:** An odds ratio represents the odds of reaching age 100 given a specific biomarker quintile compared to the odds of reaching age 100 in the median reference quintile (Q3)\[cite: 3, 7, 15\]. An OR below 1.0 indicates a lower likelihood of surviving to 100 relative to the median reference category.  
*   
* **Participants:** Of 44,636 eligible participants, 5,851 (13.1%) died before age 80; 21,234 (47.6%) died between age 80 and 89; 16,327 (36.6%) died between age 90 and 99; and 1,224 (2.7%) reached their 100th birthday.  
* 

### **Assertive Critical Appraisal**

* **Limitations & Bias (STROBE Framework):**  
* 

  * **Residual Confounding:** The primary failure mode of this study is unmeasured lifestyle and socio-behavioral confounding. Critical variables—specifically lifetime tobacco consumption, alcohol intake patterns, physical activity, dietary patterns, and socioeconomic status—were absent from the registry databases. Although hepatic enzymes (ASAT, GGT) and uric acid were hypothesized to reflect alcohol consumption, direct adjustment was not possible.  
  *   
  * **Reverse Causality & Survival Selection:** Baseline blood draws were taken between ages 64 and 99 (mean age 79.6 in future centenarians vs 76.7 in non-centenarians). Non-centenarians who died in their late 60s or 70s were closer to end-of-life pathology at the time of sampling, potentially depressing nutritional or metabolic indicators (such as total cholesterol and albumin) secondary to preclinical cachexia, chronic inflammation, or organ failure rather than normal baseline aging.  
  *   
  * **Categorization Artifacts:** Transforming continuous biomarker values into quintiles avoids non-linear modeling assumptions but introduces artificial boundaries, discarding intra-quintile variance\[cite: 3, 12\].  
  *   
* **Reporting Quality Assessment (STROBE):**  
* 

  * The study adequately accounts for cohort assembly and utilizes multiple imputation for missing baseline laboratory data.  
  *   
  * **Confounding Adjustment:** Multivariable models adjust for age at measurement (5-year bands), sex, and the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI). However, the failure to obtain or adjust for active smoking and direct alcohol consumption remains a substantial limitation under STROBE standards for observational epidemiology\[cite: 9, 12, 13\].  
  *   
* **Reporting Quality Assessment (RECORD) for RWE Studies:**  
* 

  * **Database Provenance & Linkage:** The linkage of the Central Automation Laboratory (AMORIS) cohort with Swedish national registries (National Patient Register, Cause of Death Registry, Total Population Register) via personal identification numbers is comprehensively documented.  
  *   
  * **Phenotype & Diagnostic Coding:** Hospitalization data used to calculate the 10-year lookback CCI were mapped using ICD-8 and ICD-9 codes with reference to validated Swedish registry algorithms\[cite: 3, 13\].  
  *   
  * **Data Cleaning & Attrition:** Emigrated participants (\$n \= 247\$) were documented and excluded. Laboratory methodology was centralized and standardized, minimizing inter-assay variation.  
  *   
* **Applicability:** The cohort is drawn from individuals undergoing routine outpatient health check-ups or referral laboratory testing within Stockholm County between 1985 and 1996\. While mortality trajectories match the regional population, applicability to racially or ethnically diverse populations outside Scandinavia is unconfirmed. Clinically, treating routine biomarkers in older adults solely to mirror "centenarian quintiles" (such as permissive hypercholesterolemia) is unsupported and conflates prognostic epidemiology with therapeutic intervention targets.  
* 

### **Research Objective**

To describe and compare blood-based biomarker profiles between ages 64 and 99 among individuals who eventually became centenarians versus shorter-lived peers, evaluate associations between specific biomarker quintiles and survival to age 100, and evaluate profile homogeneity within the centenarian cohort.

### **Study Design**

Prospective population-based observational cohort study with retrospective registry linkage and up to 35 years of longitudinal follow-up (mean follow-up 11.0 years). Statistical models utilized multivariable logistic regression adjusted for baseline age, sex, and comorbid disease burden (CCI), supplemented by K-median cluster analysis using the Miclust package.

### **Setting and Participants**

* **Setting:** Outpatient clinics and routine health testing facilities in Stockholm County, Sweden, with laboratory tests processed by the Central Automation Laboratories (1985–1996) and linked to nationwide administrative health registers through December 31, 2020\.  
*   
* **Eligibility:** Individuals born between 1893 and 1920 with baseline biomarker assessments measured between ages 64 and 99 (\$N \= 44,636\$ analyzed; 1,224 reached age 100). Emigrants (\$n \= 247\$) were excluded.
